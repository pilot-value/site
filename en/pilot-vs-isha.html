Key point

Hospital-employed doctors often fall below the pilot average. Move into private practice and the tables turn decisively from your forties onward, with no ceiling on income. Pilots reach their peak as captains in their fifties and sixties — and then it stops dead at 65.

LIFESTYLE

Lifestyle comparison

It is not only about salary. How you work day to day, the strain on your body and the time you get with your family all change.

Pilot

✓
Irregular rosters and time-zone work

Pre-dawn departures and late-night landings are routine. Body-clock disruption builds up easily, so managing your health is essential.

✓
Mandatory recovery after long-haul flights

Rest periods are laid down by the Civil Aeronautics Act, but accumulated fatigue is unavoidable.

✓
100–120 days off a year

The raw number of days off is generous. They often fail to line up with your family's days off, though.

+
The appeal of staying all over the world

Layovers in London, New York and Sydney are experiences nothing else can match.

!
Family time is hard to come by

You fly on public holidays, over Obon and at New Year. Missing your children's events happens.

Doctor (hospital-employed)

✓
Overnight on-call duty

Several nights on call a month. The "post-call" day of normal duties that follows is what wears people down.

✓
Heavy mental pressure

The tension of holding a patient's life directly in your hands. Litigation risk and accountability to families weigh heavily too.

✓
Call-outs at weekends

In emergency medicine, surgery and similar fields, weekend cover is the norm. It varies hugely by specialty.

+
Deepening expertise

Knowledge and technique accumulate as you get older. Board certification becomes a weapon for life.

+
A sense of contribution to the community

The direct satisfaction of seeing a patient recover, of saving a life, is hard to find in any other line of work.

RETIREMENT REALITY

The reality of retirement and later life

Pilots are shut down at 65; doctors are still working in their eighties. The difference in how you can design your retirement income is decisive.

Retirement for pilots

Under the Civil Aeronautics Act, all flying stops completely at 65. With aviation medical standards tightening, many in practice retire in their early sixties. Finding new work afterwards is difficult, and pension plus severance become the main sources of income. Severance is substantial (tens of millions of yen up to 1億円).

Age 65flying ends by law

Retirement for doctors

No statutory retirement age. With the knowledge and the skills, plenty are still practicing in their eighties. Doctors in private practice decide for themselves when to close up. For continuity of income in later life, doctors have an overwhelming advantage.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Who earns more, a pilot or a doctor? +
Pilots average 1,697万円 (MHLW) and doctors 1,338万円 (MHLW 2024), so on average the pilot comes out ahead. Doctors in private practice, at 2,000–5,000万円, overturn that. Compared with hospital-employed doctors alone the pilot has the advantage, but a doctor who can open a practice has the potential to earn far more.
Which costs more, becoming a pilot or becoming a doctor? +
Via a company-sponsored cadet program you can become a pilot at no cost at all. A private medical school costs 3,000–4,000万円 over six years. A national medical school is around 600万円, close to the cost of pilot training (1,000–2,000万円 via a private school). The cheapest way to become a pilot is the company-sponsored cadet route.
Which is easier to become, a pilot or a doctor? +
Both are extremely hard. A doctor needs to pass medical school entrance exams — deviation score (hensachi) 65–75 — plus the national licensing examination. Company-sponsored cadet selection runs at several hundred applicants per place and is one of the toughest recruitment processes there is. At a private flight school you can obtain a license as long as you can pay for it, but finding a job afterwards is a separate hurdle.
How does retirement differ between pilots and doctors? +
For pilots, retirement at 65 is fixed by law (Civil Aeronautics Act, Article 29-2). Doctors have no statutory retirement age and many are still practicing in their eighties. For continuity of income after retirement, the doctor has the advantage. Pilots have to build their later life around severance pay and the Employees' Pension.
Whose lifestyle is better, a pilot's or a doctor's? +
For pilots the challenges are irregular rosters, long stays abroad and accumulated fatigue. Doctors have night duty and a heavy mental burden. Both are tougher than an ordinary job, but pilots have the appeal of flying the world. Doctors, on the other hand, find it easier to lead a settled life rooted in one community.
